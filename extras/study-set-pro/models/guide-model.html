0. Prerequisites: Nominal vs. Real

🔮 Conceptual Framing

Before analyzing the Bank of Canada's actions, you must distinguish strictly between nominal and real variables. Monetary policy operates on nominal variables in the short run to influence real economic activity, but in the long run, nominal variables only affect nominal outcomes.

  • Nominal vs. Real GDP: Nominal GDP (\(P \times Y\)) measures the total value of output using current prices. Real GDP (\(Y\)) measures output using constant base-year prices.
  • Inflation (\(\pi\)): The percentage change in the overall price level (\(P\)) over time.
Fisher Equation

Relates nominal interest rates (\(i\)), real interest rates (\(r\)), and inflation (\(\pi\)). The standard linear approximation used in ECO102 is:

\[ i \;\approx\; r + \pi \quad \implies \quad r \;\approx\; i - \pi \]

The central bank sets the nominal rate (\(i\)), but it is the real rate (\(r\)) that ultimately determines borrowing and spending decisions in the economy.

1. Money and the Money Supply

Lecture 10 · What money is and how we measure it

1.1 Definition and Functions of Money

In economics, "money" is not synonymous with wealth or income. It is a highly specific term referring to the stock of assets that can be immediately used to execute a transaction.

Definition — Functions of Money

To be classified as money, an asset must reliably perform three functions:

  1. Medium of Exchange: It is generally accepted by buyers and sellers in trade for goods and services. It eliminates the "double coincidence of wants" required in a barter economy.
  2. Unit of Account: It provides a standardized metric for quoting prices and tracking debts.
  3. Store of Value: It retains purchasing power over time. If you hold it, it should still buy roughly the same amount of goods later.

Modern economies operate on Fiat Money. This is money that has no intrinsic value (unlike gold or cowrie shells) and cannot be freely exchanged for a precious metal. Its value is derived entirely by government decree and the collective belief that others will accept it (social consensus).

⚠️ Cryptocurrencies vs. Fiat

Why aren't cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin considered money in a strict macroeconomic sense? They fail the three functions:

  • Medium of exchange: Very few vendors accept them directly for daily goods.
  • Unit of account: Prices in stores are not quoted in BTC.
  • Store of value: High volatility means purchasing power fluctuates wildly day-to-day.

1.2 Measuring the Money Supply & Liquidity

Not all money is equally accessible. Central banks and economists categorize the money supply based on liquidity — the ease with which an asset can be converted into the economy's medium of exchange without loss of value.

  • The Monetary Base: The most liquid form of money. It is under the direct control of the central bank. It consists of physical currency in circulation PLUS bank reserves (the checking accounts that commercial banks hold at the central bank).
  • Broad Money: Includes the monetary base plus less liquid assets like commercial checking accounts, savings accounts, and time deposits. The central bank only indirectly influences broad money by manipulating the cost of bank reserves.
📝 Practice 1.1 — Fiat Money Concept Check

It holds value because the state requires it for taxes (creating a baseline demand, B) and because of collective network effects—you accept it because you know the grocery store will accept it (D). 2. The Quantity Theory of Money

Lecture 10 · Connecting money supply to output and inflation

2.1 The Velocity of Money

🔮 Intuition — The Velocity of Money

Imagine an economy with only a single $10 bill. If I use it to buy an apple from you, and you use it to buy bread from the baker, and the baker uses it to buy flour, that single $10 bill has facilitated $30 worth of transactions. The rate at which money changes hands is called the Velocity of Money.

Definition — Velocity Formula

Velocity (\(V\)) is defined mathematically as the ratio of nominal GDP to the money supply (\(M\)).

\[ V \equiv \frac{P \times Y}{M} \]

Where:

  • \(P \times Y\) = Nominal GDP (Price level \(\times\) Real Output)
  • \(M\) = Total Money Supply

2.2 The Equation of Exchange

Rearranging the velocity definition yields the foundational equation for monetary macroeconomics:

Theorem — The Equation of Exchange
\[ M \times V \;=\; P \times Y \]

This is an accounting identity. It states that the total amount of money circulating (\(M \times V\)) must equal the total nominal value of all goods and services produced (\(P \times Y\)).

2.3 Long-Run Inflation & The Classical Dichotomy

What happens if the central bank suddenly doubles the money supply?

  • Short-run: Spending rises since nominal incomes rise. Because prices are "sticky" (slow to adjust), real output (\(Y\)) might temporarily increase.
  • Long-run: Over time, since the productive capacity of the economy (\(\bar{Y}\)) has not changed, firms will simply double their prices.
Definition — The Classical Dichotomy

The principle that in the long run, nominal variables (like the money supply) do not affect real variables (like real GDP or unemployment). Real variables are determined by technology, labor, and capital. If the government added a zero to every dollar, the economy cannot produce any more real goods—only prices change.

We can express the Equation of Exchange in terms of percentage growth rates. Using the mathematical rule that the percentage change of a product is approximately the sum of the percentage changes:

\[ \text{Growth}(M) + \text{Growth}(V) \;\approx\; \text{Growth}(P) + \text{Growth}(Y) \] \[ g_M + g_V \;\approx\; \pi + g_Y \]
The Quantity Theory of Money

The Quantity Theory makes two critical long-run assumptions based on the Classical Dichotomy:

  1. Velocity depends on technology and is constant: \(g_V \approx 0\).
  2. Real GDP grows at a constant potential rate: \(g_Y = \bar{g}_y\).

Substituting these into the growth rate equation yields the fundamental determinant of long-run inflation:

\[ g_M \;\approx\; \pi + \bar{g}_y \quad \implies \quad \pi \;=\; g_M - \bar{g}_y \]

Conclusion: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." In the long run, inflation depends entirely on the growth in the money supply relative to the growth of real output.

2.4 Cumulative Problem Set: Quantity Theory

📝 Practice 2.1 — Basic Velocity Calculation

The money supply in Matango is currently 26,350. If nominal GDP is equal to 368,900, what is the velocity of money?

Show Solution

Use the definition of velocity: \( V = \dfrac{P \times Y}{M} \)

Nominal GDP is \( P \times Y = 368,900 \). The money supply is \( M = 26,350 \).

\[ V = \frac{368,900}{26,350} = \mathbf{14} \]
📝 Practice 2.2 — Shock to Velocity (Prices)

Suppose a new technology is created which greatly increases the speed that economic transactions take place, such that the velocity of money increases by 50%. If the money supply and real GDP both stay constant, what happens to the price level?

Show Solution

Start with the Equation of Exchange: \( M \times V = P \times Y \).

If \( V \) increases by a factor of 1.5, the left side of the equation becomes \( M \times (1.5 \times V) \).

Since \( M \) and \( Y \) are held constant, the right side must balance this by increasing \( P \) by the same factor.

\[ M \times (1.5 V) = (1.5 P) \times Y \]

Answer: The price level increases by 50%.

📝 Practice 2.3 — Policy Response to Velocity Shock

Continuing from Practice 2.2, what should the central bank of Matango do to prevent the price level from changing? (Recall the initial money supply was 26,350 and old velocity was 14).

Show Solution

The new velocity is \( 14 \times 1.5 = 21 \).

To keep the price level constant, the nominal GDP (\( P \times Y \)) must remain at its original value of 368,900.

We need a new money supply (\( M_{\text{new}} \)) such that: \( M_{\text{new}} \times 21 = 368,900 \).

\[ M_{\text{new}} = \frac{368,900}{21} = \mathbf{17,566.67} \]

Answer: The central bank must reduce the money supply by 1/3 (from 26,350 to 17,566.67).

📝 Practice 2.4 — Simultaneous Shocks (Exam-Level)

Suppose velocity increases by 50% (as above), but this new technology spurs real GDP to increase by 100%. If the money supply stays constant, what exactly happens to the price level?

Show Solution

Set up the initial and final states using the Equation of Exchange.

Initial: \( M_0 \times V_0 = P_0 \times Y_0 \)

Final: \( M_1 \times V_1 = P_1 \times Y_1 \)

We are given:

  • \( M_1 = M_0 \) (money supply is constant)
  • \( V_1 = 1.5 \times V_0 \) (velocity increases 50%)
  • \( Y_1 = 2 \times Y_0 \) (real GDP increases 100%, i.e., doubles)

Substitute into the final state equation:

\[ M_0 \times (1.5 \times V_0) = P_1 \times (2 \times Y_0) \] \[ 1.5 \times (M_0 \times V_0) = 2 \times P_1 \times Y_0 \]

Substitute the initial state (\( P_0 \times Y_0 \)) for (\( M_0 \times V_0 \)):

\[ 1.5 \times (P_0 \times Y_0) = 2 \times P_1 \times Y_0 \]

Cancel \( Y_0 \) from both sides:

\[ 1.5 \times P_0 = 2 \times P_1 \quad \implies \quad P_1 = \frac{1.5}{2} \times P_0 = 0.75 \times P_0 \]

The new price level is 75% of the old price level.

Answer: The price level falls by 25%.

📝 Practice 2.5 — Quantity Theory Predictions

According to the quantity theory of money, which of the following is true?

A) changes in the growth rate of money will 1-for-1 change inflation.
B) the growth rate of output depends on the growth rate of money.
C) when the inflation rate increases, the growth rate of output must fall.
D) velocity is constant over the long run.

Show Solution
Answer: D.

Reasoning: The core assumption of the Quantity Theory of Money that allows us to link money growth directly to inflation is that velocity (\(V\)) is determined by technological/institutional factors and is constant over the long run. (A) is slightly inaccurate because inflation also depends on real GDP growth (\(\pi = g_M - g_Y\)). (B) violates the Classical Dichotomy. (C) is a short-run Phillips curve phenomenon, not a long-run Quantity Theory prediction.

3. Bank of Canada & Monetary Policy Goals

Chapter 22 · Mandates, price stability, and inflation targeting

3.1 The Dual-Mandate & Inflation Targeting

Since 1991, the Bank of Canada (BoC) has focused heavily on keeping inflation low, stable, and predictable. When inflation is low and stable, it doesn't distort people's economic choices, minimizing its costs. In 2021, the BoC renewed this target but explicitly embraced a dual-mandate.

Definition — The Bank of Canada's Goals
  1. Price Stability (Primary): Keeping inflation within a 1% to 3% control range, with a strict target at the 2% mid-point.
  2. Maximum Sustainable Employment: Utilizing the flexibility of the 1% to 3% range to actively seek the maximum sustainable level of employment when conditions warrant.
🔮 Intuition — The Interdependence Principle

Why don't these two goals conflict constantly? Because inflation and unemployment are interdependent. When unemployment is below its lowest sustainable level, the economy is overheating (positive output gap). Capacity constraints cause firms to raise prices faster, pushing inflation above the 2% target.

Therefore, by targeting 2% inflation and raising rates when it exceeds that, the BoC is simultaneously cooling the economy back to its maximum sustainable employment level. There is no long-run trade-off between price stability and employment stability.

The Role of Expectations: By publicly announcing a 2% target, the BoC tries to anchor inflation expectations (\(\pi^e\)). If people and price-setters believe inflation will be 2%, they build 2% into wage contracts and price hikes. This creates a virtuous cycle that ensures inflation actually remains at 2%.

3.2 Why Not Target 0% Inflation?

If the goal is price stability, why doesn't the Bank aim for 0% inflation? Macroeconomists identify four critical reasons why a 0% target is dangerous:

  1. Inflation greases the wheels of the labour market.
    Employers hate cutting nominal wages because it ruins morale. During a recession, firms need to reduce real costs to avoid mass layoffs. If inflation is 2%, a firm can give a 0% nominal raise, which effectively cuts real wages by 2% quietly. If inflation were 0%, they would be forced to execute painful nominal wage cuts or simply fire workers. Thus, 0% inflation leads to higher unemployment during recessions.
  2. The Zero Lower Bound (ZLB) constraint.
    Central banks cannot push nominal interest rates (\(i_f\)) much below zero. Recall the Fisher equation: \(r = i - \pi\). To stimulate an economy in a deep recession, the bank needs a negative real interest rate (\(r\)).
    • If \(\pi = 2\%\) and \(i\) is cut to \(0\%\), \(r = -2\%\).
    • If \(\pi = 0\%\) and \(i\) is cut to \(0\%\), \(r = 0\%\). The bank loses critical ammunition to fight recessions.
  3. The Risk of Deflation.
    Central banks aren't perfectly precise; they often undershoot their targets. If the target is 0%, a slight miss means deflation (\(\pi < 0\)). Deflation causes people to delay spending (why buy today if it's cheaper tomorrow?). This drop in aggregate expenditure reduces output, causing prices to fall further—a vicious cycle that crushes the economy.
  4. Measured inflation may be overstated.
    Official measures of inflation fail to perfectly account for quality improvements and the introduction of new products (substitution and quality biases). Therefore, a measured inflation rate of 0% might actually mean the economy is experiencing a true inflation rate of -1% (mild deflation).

3.3 Cumulative Problem Set: Policy Goals

📝 Practice 3.1 — Policy Undershooting

Why do central banks tend to undershoot their inflation target?

A) Inflation target is set too high
B) Monetary policy is not effective at increasing inflation
C) Desire to avoid increasing inflation expectations
D) Official measures of inflation are biased upward
E) Below-target inflation guarantees maximum employment

Show Solution
Answer: C.

Reasoning: Central banks rely heavily on managing inflation expectations. If inflation creeps above the target, there is a risk that the public will expect higher inflation permanently, leading to a self-fulfilling cycle of price hikes. To avoid unanchoring expectations on the upside, central banks often treat the target more like a ceiling, resulting in a tendency to undershoot.

📝 Practice 3.2 — Short Answer: The ZLB

Explain how a 3% inflation target would give a central bank more power to fight a severe recession than a 1% inflation target.

Show Solution

During a severe recession, the central bank wants to stimulate investment and consumption by lowering the real interest rate (\(r\)). However, they can only directly control the nominal interest rate (\(i\)), which cannot drop below the Zero Lower Bound (\(i \ge 0\)).

Because \(r \approx i - \pi\), the lowest possible real rate achievable is \(r = 0 - \pi = -\pi\).

With a 1% inflation target, the lowest real rate the bank can achieve is -1%. With a 3% inflation target, the bank can push the real rate down to -3%, providing significantly more economic stimulus without violating the zero lower bound.

4. How the Bank Sets Interest Rates

Lecture 10 & Chapter 22 · The mechanics of the overnight market

4.1 The Overnight Market & Bank Reserves

🔮 Intuition — The Plumbing of the Banking System

When you buy a coffee with your debit card, money doesn't physically move; it is electronically transferred from your bank (e.g., TD) to the coffee shop's bank (e.g., Scotiabank). To settle these billions of dollars in daily transactions, commercial banks must hold highly liquid cash accounts at the Bank of Canada. These are called bank reserves.

At the end of each day, some banks have a deficit of reserves (they sent out more money than they received) and some have a surplus. Banks with deficits borrow from banks with surpluses. This borrowing happens for exactly one night. The interest rate charged on these interbank loans is the overnight rate.

The overnight rate is the fundamental baseline interest rate in the economy. The Bank of Canada (BoC) does not simply "declare" what this rate will be; it manipulates the supply and demand for reserves to force the market to trade at the BoC's desired target overnight rate (\(i_f\)).

4.2 The Operating Band: Bank Rate vs. Deposit Rate

To control the overnight rate, the BoC sets up an operating band using two standing facilities. These act as an absolute ceiling and an absolute floor for the interest rate.

Definition — The Operating Band
  • Bank Rate (The Ceiling): The rate the BoC charges commercial banks to borrow reserves directly from the central bank. No rational bank will borrow from another commercial bank at a rate higher than the Bank Rate, because they can just go to the BoC.
  • Deposit Rate (The Floor): The interest rate the BoC pays to commercial banks for storing excess reserves overnight. No rational bank will lend to another commercial bank at a rate lower than the Deposit Rate, because they can just leave the money at the BoC and earn risk-free interest.
⚠️ Critical Lecture Update: Floor System

Historically (and in older textbooks), the BoC targeted the midpoint of the operating band. For example, if the band was 2.50% to 3.00%, the target was 2.75%.

However, as per your lecture notes: "Nowadays, BoC tries to keep the overnight rate (\(i_f\)) = the deposit rate." By flooding the system with excess reserves, the supply curve shifts so far to the right that the market clears exactly at the floor. Always rely on this contemporary framework if asked conceptually.

To hit its target, the BoC shifts the supply or demand of overnight funds using these specific tools:

  1. Changing standing offers: Mechanically raising or lowering the Bank Rate and Deposit Rate.
  2. Injecting or withdrawing federal funds: The BoC acts as the federal government's banker. It can shift federal tax receipts into or out of the commercial banking system, altering the supply of reserves.
  3. Repos (Overnight Repurchase Agreements): The BoC temporarily buys bonds from banks with cash, injecting reserves to push the rate down.
  4. Reverse Repos: The BoC temporarily sells bonds to banks, draining cash out of the system to push the rate up.
  5. Open Market Operations: Outright permanent purchases or sales of government bonds (more common for Quantitative Easing, covered later).

4.3 Calculating the Effective Overnight Rate

While the BoC sets a target, the actual rate at which banks lend to each other is a market rate called the Effective Overnight Rate (often tracked as CORRA in Canada). It is the weighted average of all overnight transactions between commercial banks.

Formula — Effective Overnight Rate
\[ \text{Effective Rate} = \frac{\sum (\text{Loan Amount} \times \text{Interest Rate})}{\text{Total Borrowed}} \]

Note: You only include transactions between commercial banks. Do not include internal cash usage or reserves sitting idly at the central bank.

4.4 Cumulative Problem Set: Interest Rates

📝 Practice 4.1 — Calculating the Effective Overnight Rate

TD Bank has $8.6 million of payments to settle at the end of the day. They use $1.2 million of their own cash and borrow the remaining amount from the overnight market. From Scotiabank, TD borrows $4.4 million at an interest rate of 2.87%. From RBC, TD borrows $3.0 million at an interest rate of 2.65%.

(a) Calculate the effective overnight rate for these transactions.

(b) Suppose RBC had an additional $1.4 million in reserves stored in their account at the Bank of Canada, and the deposit rate is 2.5%. What is the new effective overnight rate?

Show Solution

Part (a):

Identify the relevant transactions. We only care about interbank borrowing. We ignore the $8.6M total payments and the $1.2M of internal cash.

Total borrowed = 4.4 + 3.0 = 7.4 million.

\[ \text{Effective Rate} = \frac{(4.4 \times 0.0287) + (3.0 \times 0.0265)}{4.4 + 3.0} \] \[ \text{Effective Rate} = \frac{0.12628 + 0.0795}{7.4} = \frac{0.20578}{7.4} \approx 0.0278 \text{ or } \mathbf{2.78\%} \]

Notice the rate is closer to 2.87% than 2.65% because a larger sum (4.4M vs 3.0M) was borrowed at the higher rate.

Part (b):

No change (2.78%). The effective overnight rate only measures active loans *between commercial banks*. Reserves sitting idly at the Bank of Canada earning the deposit rate are not part of the interbank lending market. There is no new transaction to average in.

📝 Practice 4.2 — Bank Decision Making (The Landry Loan)

Lysandre wants a $1,000 loan for a business project that will reliably pay out $1,100 next year. The bank has no excess reserves but can borrow from a local competitor at an 11% rate.

(a) What is Lysandre’s maximum willingness to pay in interest?

(b) Will the bank make the loan to Lysandre at his maximum rate?

(c) The Bank of Canada’s Bank Rate drops to 9.5%. Will the commercial bank make the loan now?

(d) Suppose the bank actually has plenty of excess reserves. The Deposit Rate at the BoC is 10.5%. Will the bank make the loan to Lysandre?

Show Solution

Part (a): Expected yield = \(\frac{1100 - 1000}{1000} = \mathbf{10\%}\). Anything higher than 10% interest causes Lysandre to lose money, so 10% is his absolute maximum.

Part (b): No. The bank's cost of funds (11%) is greater than the 10% return they would earn from loaning to Lysandre. They would lose 1% on the spread.

Part (c): Yes. The bank can now borrow directly from the BoC at the ceiling rate (Bank Rate) of 9.5%. They borrow at 9.5% and lend to Lysandre at 10%, pocketing a 0.5% profit spread.

Part (d): No. Even with cash on hand, the bank faces an opportunity cost. They can park the cash risk-free at the BoC and earn 10.5%. Loaning it to Lysandre only yields 10% and carries default risk. They will choose the deposit rate.

5. Bond Pricing and Yields

Lecture 10 · The math of fixed-income assets

5.1 Bond Terminology

A bond is simply a legal promise to repay a debt—an IOU issued by governments or corporations to raise capital.

Definition — Anatomy of a Bond
  • Principal (Face Value): The amount originally lent, which will be repaid at maturity.
  • Coupon Rate: The promised interest rate, fixed when the bond is first issued.
  • Coupon Payment: The actual dollar amount paid periodically.
    \(\text{Coupon Payment} = \text{Coupon Rate} \times \text{Principal}\).
  • Term to Maturity: The lifespan of the bond. (e.g., a 1-year bond repays the principal plus the final coupon payment exactly one year from today).
⚠️ The Crucial Difference: Coupon Rate vs. Yield

The Coupon Rate is stamped on the physical paper bond. It never changes. It determines the cash payouts.

The Yield is the actual rate of return an investor earns. Because you can buy a bond on the secondary market for a price that is different from the Principal, the yield will differ from the coupon rate. The yield fluctuates every second the market is open.

5.2 Pricing Formula and Yields

1-Year Bond Pricing Formula

For a bond maturing in exactly one year, the relationship between its market price (\(P_{Bond}\)), the yield (\(y\)), the Principal, and the Coupon Rate (\(c\)) is:

\[ P_{Bond} \times (1 + y) = \text{Principal} \times (1 + c) \]

To isolate the Yield:

\[ y = \frac{(\text{Principal} + \text{Coupon Payment}) - P_{Bond}}{P_{Bond}} \]
Example — Calculating Yields

Consider a 1-year bond with a Principal of $100 and a Coupon Rate of 5%. The payout in exactly one year will be: $100 + ($100 × 0.05) = $105.

  • If you buy the bond for exactly $100:
    Yield = \(\frac{105 - 100}{100} = 5.00\%\). (Yield = Coupon Rate)
  • If you buy the bond for a discount at $96:
    Yield = \(\frac{105 - 96}{96} = 9.375\%\). (Yield > Coupon Rate)
  • If you buy the bond for a premium at $103:
    Yield = \(\frac{105 - 103}{103} = 1.94\%\). (Yield < Coupon Rate)

5.3 The Inverse Relationship

Theorem — Bond Prices and Yields Move Inversely

As the market interest rate (yield) rises, the price of existing bonds must fall. As the market interest rate falls, the price of existing bonds must rise.

🔮 Intuition — Why Prices and Yields are Inversely Related (Arbitrage)

Suppose you hold a bond that pays a fixed 3% coupon. Suddenly, the Bank of Canada raises interest rates, and newly issued bonds are now paying a 5% coupon.

If you try to sell your 3% bond for its full face value, nobody will buy it. Why buy a bond paying $3 a year when they can buy a new one paying $5 a year? To find a buyer, you must discount the price of your bond. You must lower the price until the buyer's actual rate of return (yield) matches the new 5% market standard. Thus, higher market interest rates force existing bond prices down.

5.4 Cumulative Problem Set: Bonds

📝 Practice 5.1 — Evaluating Bond Expensiveness

You are considering purchasing one of two bonds, one from Watts’ Fine Goods or one from Rabiteman Inc. Both bonds have a principal of $500 and a term-to-maturity of one year. The bond from Watts pays a 2% coupon rate while the bond from Rabiteman pays a 17.3% coupon rate.

Given this information, is the Rabiteman bond more expensive, less expensive, or the same price as the Watts bond? By how much?

Show Solution

Since both bonds face the exact same market interest rate (assuming equal risk), they must offer the same yield to investors. However, their payouts differ drastically.

Watts payout = \( 500 + (500 \times 0.02) = 510 \).

Rabiteman payout = \( 500 + (500 \times 0.173) = 586.50 \).

Because the Rabiteman bond pays out significantly more cash at the end of the year, investors will bid its price up today compared to the Watts bond. Therefore, the Rabiteman bond is more expensive.

By how much? The price is strictly proportional to the future payout (since \(P = \text{Payout} / (1+y)\)). The ratio of payouts is \(586.50 / 510 = 1.15\). Therefore, its price is exactly 15% higher than the Watts bond.

📝 Practice 5.2 — Pricing a Bond from Market Yield

Yangus purchases a newly-issued, two-year government bond with a principal amount of $10,000 and a coupon rate of 6% paid annually. One year before the bond matures (and after receiving the coupon payment for the first year), Yangus sells the bond in the bond market. What price will Yangus receive for his bond if the current market interest rate is 5%? (Round to the nearest dollar).

Show Solution

Step 1: Determine the remaining payout. Because Yangus holds the bond until there is exactly one year left, whoever buys it is effectively buying a 1-year bond. At maturity, the bond will pay the principal plus the final coupon payment.

Final Payout = \( 10,000 + (10,000 \times 0.06) = \mathbf{10,600} \).

Step 2: Apply the pricing formula. The buyer demands a 5% yield (market interest rate).

\[ P_{Bond} = \frac{\text{Final Payout}}{1 + \text{Yield}} \] \[ P_{Bond} = \frac{10,600}{1 + 0.05} = \frac{10,600}{1.05} \approx 10,095.238 \]

Answer: Yangus will sell the bond for approximately $10,095. Because the bond's coupon (6%) is higher than the new market rate (5%), it trades at a premium (above its $10,000 face value).

📝 Practice 5.3 — Short Answer: Policy Shifts

When the Bank of Canada lowers its target interest rate from 6% to 5%, what happens to the price of existing bonds? Does this only apply to bonds with a coupon rate less than 5%?

Show Solution

When the Bank of Canada lowers interest rates, the yield demanded by the market falls. Due to the inverse relationship between yields and bond prices, the price of all existing bonds will increase.

This applies universally, regardless of the coupon rate. A 4% coupon bond becomes more attractive (price rises), and an 8% coupon bond becomes even more attractive (price rises). The entire bond market reprices upward.

6. The Transmission Mechanism & Taylor Rule

Chapter 22 · How policy rates steer the macroeconomy

6.1 Ripple Effects: From Overnight Rate to Aggregate Expenditure

When the Bank of Canada lowers the overnight rate, the effect cascades through the entire economy via the transmission mechanism.

🔮 The Transmission Chain
  1. Interbank cost falls: The cost of funds for major commercial banks drops.
  2. Retail rates fall: Applying the Cost-Benefit Principle, banks lower the rates they charge consumers (mortgages, auto loans, credit cards) and businesses (corporate loans) to capture more lending volume.
  3. Consumption & Investment rise: Following the Opportunity Cost Principle, it is now cheaper for consumers to borrow and less rewarding to save. \(C\) and \(I\) increase.
  4. Exchange Rate depreciates (NX rises): Lower Canadian interest rates make Canadian financial assets less attractive to global investors. Foreigners sell CAD, causing the Canadian Dollar to depreciate. A weaker CAD makes Canadian exports cheaper abroad and foreign imports more expensive at home. Therefore, Net Exports (\(NX\)) increase.
  5. Aggregate Expenditure & GDP rise: With \(C\), \(I\), and \(NX\) all rising, aggregate demand shifts outward, boosting real GDP and eventually pushing up inflation.

6.2 The Policy Rule-of-Thumb (The Taylor Rule)

How does the Bank of Canada decide exactly what the overnight rate should be? They look at the current state of the economy and apply a systematic guideline, often referred to as the Policy Rule-of-Thumb (or the Taylor Rule).

Definition — Neutral Real Interest Rate

The neutral real interest rate (\(r^*\)) is the real interest rate that prevails when the economy is in "Goldilocks" mode—producing exactly at its potential output (output gap = 0) with stable inflation. It neither stimulates nor slows down the economy.

Formula — The Policy Rule-of-Thumb

The Bank evaluates nominal rates by stripping out inflation to focus on the real rate. The formula used to target the nominal overnight rate is:

\[ \text{Overnight Rate} - \text{Inflation} = \text{Neutral Real Rate} + \frac{1}{2}(\text{Inflation Gap}) + \text{Output Gap} \]

Where:

  • Inflation Gap = \(\text{Actual Inflation} - \text{Target Inflation (2\%)}\).
  • Output Gap = The percentage difference between actual output and potential output. (Positive means overheating, negative means recession).

Rearranging to solve for the nominal Overnight Rate gives:

\[ \text{Overnight Rate} = \text{Neutral Real Rate} + \text{Inflation} + \frac{1}{2}(\text{Inflation} - 2\%) + \text{Output Gap} \]
Example — Applying the Rule

Assume the neutral real interest rate is 1%. Actual inflation is currently 1.5%, and the output gap is +0.5% (meaning output is 0.5% above potential). What should the nominal overnight rate be?

Step 1: Calculate the desired Real Rate

\[ \text{Real Rate} = 1\% + \frac{1}{2}(1.5\% - 2.0\%) + 0.5\% \] \[ \text{Real Rate} = 1\% + \frac{1}{2}(-0.5\%) + 0.5\% = 1\% - 0.25\% + 0.5\% = \mathbf{1.25\%} \]

Step 2: Convert to Nominal Rate (Fisher Equation)

\[ \text{Nominal Rate} = \text{Real Rate} + \text{Inflation} \] \[ \text{Nominal Rate} = 1.25\% + 1.5\% = \mathbf{2.75\%} \]

Rules vs. Discretion: The rule provides a predictable baseline, but central bank decisions are systematic, not automatic. The Governing Council uses discretion to deviate from the rule when faced with unusual shocks (e.g., forecasting a looming recession, financial instability, or global pandemics) to "get ahead of the curve."

6.3 Cumulative Problem Set: Transmission & Rules

📝 Practice 6.1 — Exchange Rate Dynamics

Trace the exact sequence of events that leads from an increase in the Bank of Canada's overnight rate to a change in the quantity of goods demanded by foreigners.

Show Solution

1. The BoC raises the overnight rate.

2. Canadian commercial banks raise their interest rates, increasing the return on Canadian financial assets (like savings accounts and bonds).

3. Global investors seek the highest risk-adjusted returns, so they shift capital into Canada to capture these higher yields.

4. To invest in Canada, they must buy Canadian Dollars. This increases the global demand for CAD.

5. The Canadian Dollar appreciates in value relative to foreign currencies.

6. Because the CAD is now stronger, it takes more foreign currency to buy the same Canadian-priced good. Consequently, Canadian exports become more expensive to foreigners, and the quantity of goods demanded by foreigners decreases (Net Exports fall).

📝 Practice 6.2 — Rule of Thumb Calculation

The economy enters a severe recession. The output gap falls to -3.0%. Inflation drops to 0.5%. Assuming the neutral real interest rate is historically estimated at 1.0%, what overnight rate does the policy rule-of-thumb prescribe?

Show Solution

Apply the formula:

\[ \text{Desired Real Rate} = \text{Neutral Rate} + \frac{1}{2}(\text{Inflation} - 2\%) + \text{Output Gap} \] \[ \text{Desired Real Rate} = 1.0\% + \frac{1}{2}(0.5\% - 2.0\%) + (-3.0\%) \] \[ \text{Desired Real Rate} = 1.0\% - 0.75\% - 3.0\% = \mathbf{-2.75\%} \]

Now, convert to nominal to find the prescribed overnight rate:

\[ \text{Nominal Rate} = \text{Desired Real Rate} + \text{Inflation} \] \[ \text{Nominal Rate} = -2.75\% + 0.5\% = \mathbf{-2.25\%} \]

Note on reality: The formula tells the BoC to set a negative nominal interest rate (-2.25%). Because of the Zero Lower Bound, the BoC cannot physically lower the rate this far. This forces them to hit 0% and pivot to Unconventional Monetary Policy (like QE), which leads directly into the next topic.

7. Unconventional Monetary Policy

Chapter 22 & Lecture 10 · What the Bank does when standard tools break

7.1 The Zero Lower Bound Context

In standard economic times, the Bank of Canada stimulates a slumping economy by lowering the short-term nominal overnight rate. However, during severe crises (like the 2008 Financial Crisis or the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic), the required rate cuts can push the overnight rate to zero.

🔮 Intuition — The Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)

The central bank faces a hard physical constraint: it cannot effectively set a deeply negative nominal interest rate. If they charged a -3% interest rate on deposits, savers would simply withdraw their money as physical cash and hide it under a mattress (earning 0% instead of losing 3%). When the overnight rate hits 0% but the economy remains below potential, the Bank must deploy unconventional tools to stimulate spending.

When the short-term rate is jammed at zero, the Bank shifts its focus to pushing down longer-term interest rates (like 5-year auto loans or 25-year home mortgages).

7.2 Forward Guidance

The simplest unconventional tool is communication.

Definition — Forward Guidance

Forward Guidance is the strategy of explicitly committing to keeping short-term interest rates low for a prolonged period in the future. By shaping market expectations, the Bank influences long-term interest rates today.

Mechanics: A 5-year loan can be thought of as a series of five 1-year loans chained together. If the Bank promises that short-term rates will stay at 0% for the next 5 years, banks are willing to charge much lower interest rates on 5-year loans today. If the public believes the commitment, long-term borrowing costs fall, spurring housing and investment spending.

7.3 Quantitative Easing (QE)

When talk isn't enough, the Bank directly intervenes in the long-term bond market.

Definition — Quantitative Easing (QE)

Quantitative Easing is the strategy of purchasing large quantities of longer-term government bonds and other securities in the open market, injecting newly created electronic money into the financial system.

The Mechanics of QE (Bond Pricing Framework)

Recall the inverse relationship between bond prices and yields from Topic 5. The transmission of QE works as follows:

  1. The Bank of Canada buys massive amounts of long-term bonds.
  2. This massive new demand shifts the demand curve for bonds to the right.
  3. Bond prices rise dramatically.
  4. Because bond prices and yields are inversely related, the yields (interest rates) on long-term bonds decrease.
  5. Lower yields on safe government bonds force investors to seek returns in riskier assets (corporate bonds, mortgages). This lowers the user cost of capital for firms and the mortgage rates for households, increasing investment (\(I\)) and consumption (\(C\)).

Note: The textbook also explains this using the loanable funds market. Buying long-term bonds effectively increases the supply of long-term loans, pushing down the equilibrium long-term interest rate.

7.4 Lender of Last Resort & Moral Hazard

A central bank's final major responsibility is maintaining the stability of the broader financial system.

Bank Runs: Banks operate on fractional reserves. If all depositors panic and demand their cash simultaneously, the bank will fail, even if it is fundamentally solvent. Bank runs are highly contagious.

Definition — Lender of Last Resort

During a financial panic, the central bank provides emergency short-term loans to financial institutions that cannot secure funding elsewhere, preventing solvent but illiquid banks from collapsing and halting the panic contagion.

⚠️ The "Too Big To Fail" Dilemma & Moral Hazard

By acting as a lender of last resort and bailing out massive, interconnected financial institutions (like AIG in 2008), the central bank creates a severe incentive problem.

Moral Hazard: If large banks know the central bank will save them from bankruptcy to prevent economic chaos ("too big to fail"), they have an incentive to take on excessive, reckless risks to maximize profits. The standard solution to this problem is heavy financial regulation: forcing banks to hold more capital so they don't need bailouts in the first place.

If the money supply grows by 5 percent per year, velocity grows by 1 percent per year, and real GDP grows by 3 percent per year, then what is the approximate annual rate of inflation? (Provide your calculation).

\[ g_M + g_V \approx \pi + g_Y \]

📝 Question 3: Bond Arbitrage and Yields

One year before maturity, the price of a bond with a principal amount of $1,000 and a coupon rate of 5 percent paid annually fell to $981.31. What must the current market interest rate (yield) be? Answer in percentage terms, rounded to two decimal places.

Show Grading Feedback

Correct Answer: 7.00%.

Analysis: The buyer acquires the bond with one year left, so it is treated as a 1-year bond for pricing purposes. Find the final payout first:

\[ \text{Payout} = \text{Principal} + (\text{Principal} \times \text{Coupon Rate}) = 1,000 + 50 = 1,050 \]

Now apply the inverse yield pricing formula:

\[ P_{Bond} \times (1 + y) = \text{Payout} \] \[ 981.31 \times (1 + y) = 1,050 \] \[ 1 + y = \frac{1,050}{981.31} \approx 1.07 \] \[ y = 0.07 \text{ or } \mathbf{7.00\%} \]

📝 Question 5: The Taylor Rule Implementation

An economy's neutral real interest rate is estimated to be 1.5%. Due to supply chain shocks, inflation is running at 4.0% (while the target is 2.0%). The economy is overheating, with an output gap of +2.0%. According to the Bank of Canada's policy rule-of-thumb, what should the nominal overnight rate be set to?

Show Grading Feedback

Correct Answer: 8.5%.

Analysis: First, determine the desired real interest rate using the rule:

\[ r_{target} = r^* + 0.5(\pi - \pi_{target}) + \text{Output Gap} \] \[ r_{target} = 1.5\% + 0.5(4.0\% - 2.0\%) + 2.0\% \] \[ r_{target} = 1.5\% + 0.5(2.0\%) + 2.0\% = 1.5\% + 1.0\% + 2.0\% = 4.5\% \]

📝 Question 6: Transmission Constraints

Explain structurally why a severe deflationary cycle (negative inflation) disables standard monetary policy transmission, using the Fisher Equation in your answer.

Show Grading Feedback

Analysis for full marks:

Standard monetary policy relies on lowering the real interest rate (\(r\)) to stimulate \(C\) and \(I\). By the Fisher equation, \(r = i - \pi\). The central bank only controls the nominal rate (\(i\)), and it faces the Zero Lower Bound (\(i \ge 0\)).

If deflation takes hold (e.g., \(\pi = -3\%\)), the lowest possible real interest rate achievable by conventional means is:

\[ r = 0\% - (-3\%) = +3\% \]

Even at a nominal rate of 0%, the real borrowing cost is 3%. The central bank cannot produce negative real interest rates. This disables standard transmission, rendering the central bank powerless to stimulate the economy without resorting to unconventional tools like Quantitative Easing.

9. One-Page Master Cheat Sheet: Monetary Policy

Printable reference for definitions, formulas, and causal chains
💰 1. Money & Money Supply

Three Functions: Medium of exchange, Unit of account, Store of value.

Fiat Money: No intrinsic value; backed by state decree and social consensus. Cryptocurrencies fail as money (poor medium of exchange/unit of account, high volatility).

Measurement:

  • Monetary Base: Currency in circulation + Bank Reserves (direct BoC control).
  • Broad Money: Base + Checking/Savings accounts (indirect BoC control via interest rates limiting bank lending).
📊 2. Quantity Theory of Money

Velocity (\(V\)): Average value of transactions supported by $1. \(V = \frac{P \times Y}{M}\).

Equation of Exchange: \(M \times V = P \times Y\) (Money Supply \(\times\) Velocity = Nominal GDP).

Classical Dichotomy: In the long run, nominal variables (\(M\)) don't affect real variables (\(Y\)).

Long-Run Inflation Formula: \(\pi \approx g_M - g_Y\) (Assuming \(g_V = 0\)). Inflation is heavily dependent on excessive money growth.

🎯 3. BoC Mandates & Targets

Dual-Mandate: 1. Price stability (2% inflation target inside a 1-3% band). 2. Maximum sustainable employment.

Why not 0% target? 1. Causes nominal wage rigidity issues (prevents disguised real wage cuts). 2. Hits the Zero Lower Bound (ZLB) faster during recessions. 3. Risks vicious deflationary spirals. 4. Measurement bias (CPI overstates inflation by ~0.5-1%).

⚙️ 4. Setting Interest Rates

The Overnight Rate (\(i_f\)): The cost to borrow reserves overnight between commercial banks.

Operating Band:

  • Bank Rate (Ceiling): Cost to borrow directly from BoC.
  • Deposit Rate (Floor): Interest paid by BoC to banks on excess reserves.

Modern execution: The BoC supplies excess reserves so the effective overnight rate equals the Deposit Rate.

Effective Rate Calculation: Weighted average of transaction volumes. (Ignore non-interbank numbers like internal cash or central bank reserves).

📉 5. Bond Pricing

Terminology: Principal = Face value; Coupon Rate = Fixed interest % printed on bond; Yield = Actual market rate of return.

1-Year Formula: \(P_{Bond} = \frac{\text{Principal} + \text{Coupon Payment}}{1 + \text{Yield}}\)

Inverse Relationship: When market interest rates (yields) RISE, existing bond prices FALL.

🌐 6. Transmission Mechanism & Rules

Chain Reaction of an Interest Rate Cut (\(i_f \downarrow\)):

  1. Commercial lending rates fall.
  2. Opportunity cost of spending drops \(\implies\) \(C \uparrow\), \(I \uparrow\).
  3. Canadian yields drop \(\implies\) foreign capital leaves \(\implies\) CAD depreciates \(\implies\) Exports get cheaper \(\implies\) \(NX \uparrow\).
  4. Aggregate expenditure rises, output gap closes, inflation pushes up.

Policy Rule-of-Thumb (Taylor Rule):

\(i_f = \text{Neutral Real Rate } (r^*) + \pi + 0.5(\pi - 2\%) + \text{Output Gap}\)

🚨 7. Unconventional Policy

Zero Lower Bound (ZLB): Nominal rates hit ~0%. Standard policy is paralyzed.

Forward Guidance: Promise to keep rates low long-term to push down long-term yields via expectations.

Quantitative Easing (QE): BoC buys massive amounts of long-term bonds \(\implies\) demand shifts right \(\implies\) bond prices surge \(\implies\) long-term yields crash \(\implies\) stimulates investment when short rates are stuck at 0%.

Lender of Last Resort: BoC provides emergency liquidity to stop bank runs. Creates Moral Hazard (too-big-to-fail banks take excessive risks), necessitating regulation.
